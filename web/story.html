A story told with Ichnic's data · October 2026

What the record catches

Every wildlife seizure leaves a trace in the public record. The record keeps some traces and loses others, and what it keeps tells us as much about the people watching as about the trade itself. This story follows the gaps.

Tarun K. Verma · Figures update daily from the Ichnic atlas and the sources it cites.

Scroll

1 · A drop that was not a drop

In 2020, recorded offences in Ranthambhore fell by more than half.

Offence records for the Ranthambhore tiger reserve in India went from 225 in 2019 to 98 in 2020. Read alone, the numbers say that offending fell. It did not: during the COVID-19 lockdowns, fewer cases were registered, because fewer people were registering them.

So a record of enforcement measures two things at once: the crime, and the effort spent looking for it. Global reporting on biodiversity has the same weakness, as it rests mostly on what governments report about themselves (Milner-Gulland et al., 2026).

Keep that in mind, because the rest of this story is built on records of exactly this kind.

2 · What is caught

The public record holds 2,264 cases, and parrots lead it.

Ichnic reads news, government releases and court pages in many languages, keeps only reports of an enforcement event, and merges reports of the same event into one case. Parrots and songbirds come first, with 343 cases, then turtles and tortoises and elephant ivory.

This is a ranking of what was caught and written about. It is not a ranking of what is traded most, and the next step shows where the two part ways.

3 · What is missed in plain sight

Plants are trafficked far more than they are reported.

In the seizures governments report to CITES, plants make up 15% of shipments. In the news cases Ichnic collects, they make up 10%. Rosewood, orchids, cacti and medicinal roots are seized often and written about rarely.

We think the reason is attention rather than enforcement: a pangolin makes a story, a container of timber seldom does. Whatever the cause, a reader who learns about wildlife crime from the news learns a version with most of its plants missing.

4 · Where the trade moves

The busiest seized route outside the United States runs from India to China.

Government records draw the trade's shape better than news does. Leaving the United States aside, India to China is the busiest seized route, with 333 shipments since 2015; 314 of them were red sanders, a timber that grows wild only in southern India, mostly shipped as logs.

The United States sits at one end of 83% of all seized shipments in these records. That does not make it the centre of the trade: it reports its seizures far more completely than other countries do, which is the Ranthambhore lesson again, at the scale of nations.

5 · Where almost no one is looking

Thousands of adverts, and almost no cases.

ECO-SOLVE, a monitoring project, recorded 30,934 online adverts for wildlife between April 2024 and September 2026. Bears offered for sale in Thailand drew 1,303 adverts; in the same period, the public record holds no case of a bear seized in Thailand.

An advert is not proof of a sale, and a missing case is not proof of no enforcement. Still, a market this visible with so little recorded enforcement is where the record is quietest, and where a researcher or a regulator would learn the most.

6 · What reaches a court

For every conviction in the record, there are about ten seizures.

The record holds 1,351 seizures, 585 arrest reports and 130 convictions. Some of that gap is real attrition, as cases collapse or never reach a court. Some is silence, because a seizure makes the news on the day and a verdict two years later often does not.

We cannot separate the two from public reports alone. That is itself the finding: the outcome that matters most for deterrence is the one the open record shows least.

7 · What even a careful record misses

Ichnic is right about what it shows, and still misses most events.

In a blind audit of 200 randomly drawn cases, 193 were real enforcement events (96.5%). Precision answers one question only. To ask how much is missed, we compared Ichnic with an independent, hand-checked list of 576 events. After removing matches that happen by chance, Ichnic found about one in five (20% to 25%).

The figure tells a reader to treat every count in this story as a floor. It also tells funders and agencies where the record is thinnest: government releases outside the big news markets, and languages the tools do not yet read.

Reading the gaps

Taken together, the steps make one argument. The open record of wildlife crime is a record of where people look: newsrooms look at charismatic animals, agencies report what they can, and courts are rarely followed to the end. So the gaps are not noise to be cleaned away. They show where attention, reporting and enforcement fall short, which makes them the most useful part of the record.

Each reader can act on a different gap. A journalist can ask what happened in court, months after the seizure. A researcher can study plants and online markets, where the record is quietest. An agency can publish its own outcomes, so that a conviction is as visible as a seizure. Ichnic keeps every number with its source and its limit, so that each of these starts from evidence rather than from the loudest story.
