Risk pathways

Wildlife crime as an environment-to-security risk: seven links, and the evidence for each

1,246 seizures in the news, 24,786 seized shipments reported to CITES, 19,346 imports seized at US ports, and 111 convictions. That last number is the story: nature is taken and moved at scale, and the record goes quiet where money and accountability begin.

The short answer

  • Wildlife crime reaches security through seven links, from extraction to crime revenue, disease risk and the erosion of ecosystems.
  • 3 links are well evidenced in open data; 1 is a gap: who profits, and how much (it funds crime and corrupts institutions).
  • Plants are 15% of CITES seizures but 10% of news cases: the pathway is greener than the headlines.
  • Security framing must not become militarisation: evidence first, rights respected, no person named.
1

Nature is taken: poaching, illegal logging and collection

Open evidence: strong

What WildTrace shows. 2,098 seizures, arrests, convictions and rescues from public reports across 40 species groups, and 24,786 seized shipments reported to CITES since 2015. Plants make up 15% of the CITES seizures but only 10% of news cases: timber, orchids and medicinal plants are traded far more than they are reported.

What the number cannot tell you. A seizure is an enforcement event. It shows that something was intercepted, not how much was taken.

Research agenda. R1.11 Interacting drivers of nature loss; R1.37 illegal extraction

2

It moves: routes, transit hubs and online markets

Open evidence: partial

What WildTrace shows. 5,697 CITES seizures passed through a third country between origin and seizure. 77 news cases span more than one country. Where reports name a transport mode, air (49) and online sale (40) lead, then road (21) and sea (4). Where online trade reached enforcement, reports most often name WhatsApp (5), Facebook (5), Instagram (3).

What the number cannot tell you. Only 2 news cases name both ends of a route, so routes come from trade records, not from reporting. Online adverts themselves are monitored by others (ECO-SOLVE, WILDTRADE); WildTrace records where online trade met enforcement.

3

It reaches the border: what inspectors intercept

Open evidence: strong

What WildTrace shows. US wildlife inspectors seized 19,346 import records of the groups WildTrace follows, from 190 countries of origin; most often from China (2,018), Indonesia (1,863), Mexico (1,801). Routes into the United States make up 83% of CITES seizure records.

What the number cannot tell you. This measures where inspection is strong and reported, above all in the United States, as much as where trade flows. The US and CITES records overlap: compare them, never add them.

4

It funds crime and corrupts institutions

Open evidence: a gap

What WildTrace shows. The record thins sharply after the seizure: 1,246 seizures, 569 arrest reports (1,761 people, counted and never named) and 111 convictions. Documented links between wildlife products and armed groups exist for specific places and periods (Douglas & Alie 2014), but the open evidence on who profits, and how much, is thin and contested (Duffy 2015; Massé et al. 2020).

What the number cannot tell you. News reports convictions far less often than seizures, so the drop is partly a reporting gap. Financial flows are not in open data; they need financial intelligence that WildTrace does not have.

Research agenda. R1.37 environmental crime as revenue for hostile actors; R5.2-R5.3 animal life and revenue-generating uses in the weaponisation of nature

5

It carries disease risk

Open evidence: partial

What WildTrace shows. Viruses confirmed by sequencing or isolation are recorded in 23 of the traded species groups (VIRION). WHO published 2,118 outbreak reports on diseases with an animal reservoir, 714 of them spread by contact with wildlife.

What the number cannot tell you. The two layers share a map, not a cause: an outbreak near a seizure does not mean the trade caused it, and virus records follow where scientists have sampled.

6

It erodes critical natural infrastructure

Open evidence: partial

What WildTrace shows. The groups seized most often include seed dispersers (hornbills, primates), predators (big cats, sharks, owls), reef builders (corals) and forest trees (rosewood, agarwood, sandalwood). About one in five land vertebrate species is traded (Scheffers et al. 2019), and ivory poaching alone drove the decline of African elephants (Wittemyer et al. 2014).

What the number cannot tell you. WildTrace records removals, not their ecological effect. The consequence for ecosystems comes from the literature, species by species.

Research agenda. Theme I, critical natural infrastructure (R1.3 cascading risks, R1.12 actionable indicators)

7

It is fought with evidence, or with rumour

Open evidence: strong

What WildTrace shows. Every case is graded: 230 rest on a government, customs, police or court source, 354 on two or more independent outlets, and 1,514 on a single report, which WildTrace treats as a lead, not a finding.

What the number cannot tell you. Grades describe the sources, not the truth of every detail in them.

Where the evidence runs out

The gaps are the research agenda. Open data cannot yet show who profits from wildlife crime, how much wildlife is taken rather than seized, or how removals cascade through ecosystems. Language technology can narrow some gaps: extracting several events from one report, telling a sale from a condemnation or a news story, and checking claims against evidence. Read the open research agenda and pick a question.

Security without militarisation

Calling wildlife crime a security threat can bring money and attention. It has also justified shoot-on-sight policies, the exclusion of local people from land they depend on, and "war by conservation" (Duffy 2015; Lunstrum 2014). WildTrace takes the security frame with three commitments: every claim carries its evidence and its limits; enforcement is counted, never glorified, and no accused person is named; and the harms to people who live alongside wildlife are part of the pathway, not a side effect.

What can be done

The research agenda sorts interventions into six kinds. For this pathway they look like this:

  • Information and foresight: open observatories that grade evidence, like this one, and early warning for new species and routes.
  • Regulation and standards: due diligence for timber and wildlife supply chains, and consistent seizure reporting by every CITES Party.
  • Capacity-building: customs, port and postal inspection where routes converge, and forensic identification of seized products.
  • Governance and coordination: environment, health and security departments reading the same evidence, not three silos.
  • Incentives: livelihoods for the communities who live with wildlife, so that protection pays more than extraction.
  • Communication: local cases linked to the system they belong to, uncertainty stated as a range, and public concern made visible.

Questions people ask

Is illegal wildlife trade a national security issue?

It reaches national security through several pathways: organised crime and corruption, disease risk, and the loss of species that ecosystems depend on. WildTrace maps seven links from nature to security and rates the open evidence for each: 3 are well evidenced in open data, and the link to crime revenue is the weakest. The 2026 Oxford research agenda on environment and national security names environmental crime as a revenue source for hostile actors (R1.37).

Does wildlife trafficking fund armed groups or terrorism?

Documented links exist for particular places and periods, such as ivory and armed groups in Central Africa (Douglas & Alie 2014). But open evidence on who profits and how much is thin, and researchers warn that overstating a poaching-terrorism nexus has been used to justify militarised conservation that harms local people (Duffy 2015; Massé et al. 2020).

How much wildlife is seized at borders?

CITES Parties reported 24,786 seized shipments of the species groups WildTrace follows since 2015, and US inspectors seized 19,346 wildlife import records between 2000 and 2022. The United States reports far more completely than most countries, so these numbers show inspection effort as much as trade.

Can wildlife trade cause pandemics?

Traded wildlife can carry viruses: 23 of the traded species groups have viruses confirmed in VIRION. A shared location between a seizure and an outbreak does not show that the trade caused it; WildTrace shows the two together as a shared map, not a cause.

Where can I get open data on wildlife seizures?

WildTrace publishes cases, CITES seizure flows and US port seizures as CSV at https://tarunv13.github.io/wildtrace/data/, with a DOI on Zenodo (10.5281/zenodo.22902819). Case data are CC BY 4.0; CITES-derived files follow the CITES terms.
